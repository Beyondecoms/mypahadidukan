The Ashwagandha Plant and Why the Root Matters

The ashwagandha plant is a small shrub, and different parts of it end up in different products. If you're buying pure ashwagandha root powder, the label should identify Withania somnifera root. 

"Ashwagandha powder" alone doesn't tell you whether you're getting root, leaves or a mix.

Here's how the common terms compare:

  • Root powder: Dried, powdered Withania somnifera root.

  • Root extract: Root processed through extraction. It is not the same as plain powder.

  • Leaf or aerial-part extract: Made from above-ground parts rather than the root.

  • Root-and-leaf blend: Contains both parts and should name them on the label.

  • Plant part not stated: You cannot tell what's inside.

This difference also explains why dosage advice online varies so much. Most supplements in clinical research use concentrated root extracts, and some use root-and-leaf blends, and the root and leaf have different chemical make-ups.

So the amounts used in studies don't translate directly to a spoonful of plain root powder in your tea.


Important Updates on Ashwagandha Usage

On 15 April 2026, India's Ministry of AYUSH directed that only ashwagandha roots be used in AYUSH drugs and products, citing higher levels of reactive withanolides such as withaferin-A in the leaves, and asked manufacturers to declare the plant part on the label.

A day later, FSSAI issued an advisory against using ashwagandha leaves in food products. Some manufacturers have challenged this, and the Karnataka High Court granted an interim stay that applies only to the petitioners while the case continues.

Either way, root-only is the simplest choice for shoppers.

Ashwagandha Benefits and What the Research Actually Shows

Why go through the trouble of simmering a root for fifteen minutes?

Because for many people, the calm it's traditionally known for is worth the wait. Ashwagandha root has long been valued in Ayurveda as a Rasayana, or rejuvenating tonic, especially for strength and vitality. Today, it's one of the more studied herbs for everyday stress and sleep.

Benefit

What research and tradition suggest

Stress

May help the body manage everyday stress. A 2025 meta-analysis found ashwagandha lowered cortisol, but its effect on how stressed people felt was not significant, so results are mixed.

Sleep quality

May help improve sleep. A meta-analysis of five trials found a small but significant improvement, with larger effects in people with insomnia and at doses of 600 mg a day or more of extract.

Calm and mood

Traditionally used to promote relaxation. Several studies suggest ashwagandha extracts might help reduce stress and anxiety.

Male reproductive health

Early research, mostly small studies in men with fertility concerns, suggests it may improve sperm quality and testosterone. The authors say larger trials are needed.

Strength and recovery

Traditionally used for strength and wellbeing. A review of small trials in healthy adults found it may support strength, power and recovery alongside exercise.

Mental clarity

Early research only. Some trial participants have reported better memory and focus, but these were subjective reports, and more studies are needed.

A quick note on the research: Most clinical studies use concentrated root extracts at set doses, usually for 4 to 12 weeks. A cup of ashwagandha tea is gentler, so think of it as part of a calming routine rather than a substitute for a studied supplement. Effects also vary from person to person.

How to Make Ashwagandha Tea the Traditional Way

In Ayurveda, tough roots are usually simmered as a kwath, or decoction, rather than steeped like leaf tea. If you're new to herbal brews, our guide to Herbal tea benefits you should know about is a good primer.

You'll need:

  • 1 cup water or milk

  • 1 tsp ashwagandha root powder (about 2-3 g), or the serving on your product label

  • Optional: honey, lemon, cinnamon, cardamom or turmeric

Method:

  1. Bring the water to a boil and add the powder.

  2. Lower the heat, cover and simmer for 10–15 minutes.

  3. Strain through a fine mesh strainer.

  4. Add spices and stir in honey once the tea has cooled slightly.

Quick option: Warm a cup of milk, stir in the powder and spices, and sweeten to taste.

Flavour variations:

  • Golden milk: Gently simmer (don't boil) the powder in milk with turmeric and a pinch of black pepper.

  • Green tea blend: Add a pinch of powder to green tea. Green tea contains caffeine, so keep this one for daytime.

  • Honey lemon: Lemon cuts the bitterness, and a spoon of winter white honey rounds it out. For the classic version without ashwagandha, see our honey lemon tea guide.

How to Use Ashwagandha Powder in Everyday Recipes

Ashwagandha tastes bitter and earthy. Creamy bases, natural sweetness and warming spices balance it best. Use the serving size on your product label in each recipe.

  • Ashwagandha milk: Stir the powder into warm (not boiling) milk with cinnamon and cardamom. Add honey once it has cooled slightly.

  • Coffee: Mix the powder into a little warm milk first, then add it to your favourite brew of Himalayan coffee.

  • Smoothie: Blend with banana, milk and a little cocoa or honey.

  • Ghee: Warm a spoon of Pahadi Badri Cow Ghee gently, take it off the heat and stir in the powder.

  • Chocolate bites: Mix with cocoa, nut butter and dates, roll into balls and refrigerate.

  • Everyday foods: Stir into warm oats, finished soups (off the heat), bold salad dressings or sweet fruit juice.

Ashwagandha for Skin Through Diet and Topical Care

Stress often shows up on your skin.

Research links psychological stress to psoriasis flare-ups, and stress hormones appear to slow the skin barrier's recovery.

From within: Since ashwagandha may help the body manage stress, it may indirectly support healthier-looking skin. Think "beauty from within," though direct studies on oral ashwagandha and skin are still limited.

On the skin: In a small 60-day trial in adults with sun-damaged skin, a lotion with 8% standardised ashwagandha root extract improved skin hydration and elasticity compared with a placebo lotion. The extract's supplier provided the product, and the authors call for larger studies.

Ways to use it:

  • Drink it: Ashwagandha tea or milk, as part of your evening wind-down.

  • Face mask: Mix the powder with honey or yoghurt for a simple mask.

  • Skincare: Look for creams or serums that list ashwagandha root extract.

  • Oil: Ashwagandha oil is traditionally used in Ayurvedic massage.

Patch-test any new mask or oil on a small area of skin first. Home-made powder masks haven't been studied the way formulated extracts have.

Ashwagandha Dosage and the Best Time to Take It

How much: For plain ashwagandha root powder, about 1 teaspoon (roughly 2-3 g) a day is a common, gentle serving for tea or warm milk. For comparison, Health Canada's monograph for traditional Ayurvedic use lists 2-6 g of dried root a day for adults. One stress study reviewed by the NIH used whole-root granules equal to about 6 g of root powder daily.

Start low: Begin with half a teaspoon for the first week, then move to a full teaspoon if you feel fine. Higher amounts can make side effects like loose stools, nausea or drowsiness more likely.

Powder isn't extract: Most studies use concentrated extracts, typically about 120-600 mg a day and occasionally up to 1,250 mg. These amounts don't convert directly to plain powder, so don't use an extract dose as a guide for your tea.

How long: Ashwagandha appears well tolerated for up to about 3 months, but there's limited data on longer use. If you'd like to continue beyond that, decide with your doctor.

When to take it: Research on timing is limited, so try it in the evening if sleep is your goal, or earlier in the day for daytime stress. If it doesn't suit you, stop.

Note: If you're pregnant, breastfeeding, have a medical condition or take regular medication, check with your doctor before use.

How to Choose the Best Ashwagandha Powder in India

The best ashwagandha powder in India isn't the one with the loudest label. Words like "pure," "natural" or "premium" don't tell you much. Check these details instead.

Source

On the Label

  • Botanical name: Withania somnifera

  • Plant part: Root. Ayush rules ask manufacturers to declare the plant part used

  • Ingredients: Ashwagandha root only, with no fillers or added herbs

  • Product type: Powder or extract (they aren't the same)

  • Traceability: Net weight, batch number and manufacturer details

In the Lab Report

Look for a batch-specific report, ideally from an independent lab, that covers:

  • Botanical identity (HPTLC or HPLC)

  • Heavy metals (lead, arsenic, cadmium, mercury)

  • Microbial contamination (E. coli, Salmonella)

  • Pesticide residues

  • Withanolide content, which matters mainly for extracts

At Home

Good root powder is usually beige to light brown, with a fine texture and a strong earthy smell. Home checks can flag obvious problems, but they can't confirm purity.

Red flags:

  • Product details that don't match across the title, label and description

  • A hidden or missing ingredient panel

  • Vague claims like "100% authentic" with no test report

  • No plant part stated

Ashwagandha Side Effects and Who Should Avoid It

Ashwagandha is generally well tolerated for up to about 3 months, but it isn't right for everyone.

Possible side effects: Stomach upset, loose stools, nausea and drowsiness. Rarely, ashwagandha has been linked to liver injury, usually appearing 2 to 12 weeks after starting a product and mostly resolving after stopping it.

Avoid it, or check with your doctor first, if you:

  • Are pregnant or breastfeeding

  • Have a thyroid condition, since ashwagandha may affect thyroid hormone levels

  • Have an autoimmune condition, which ashwagandha may trigger or worsen, according to Memorial Sloan Kettering

  • Have hormone-sensitive prostate cancer, because it may raise testosterone

  • Have liver disease or stomach ulcers

  • Have surgery coming up, since it can add to the drowsiness of sedating medicines. Tell your doctor about any supplement you take

  • Are buying for a child. Traditional dosing guidance is for adults, so ask a paediatrician first

Medicines it may interact with: Thyroid hormones, medicines for diabetes and blood pressure, immunosuppressants, sedatives, and anticonvulsants or benzodiazepines.

Stop and see a doctor if you notice yellowing of the skin or eyes, itching, unusual tiredness, nausea or abdominal discomfort. These can be signs of liver trouble.

FAQs on Ashwagandha Powder

Can we use ashwagandha without a doctor's advice?

Many healthy adults use ashwagandha root powder in food and drinks, and it's generally well tolerated short-term. If you're pregnant, have a health condition or take medicines, consult your doctor first.

Can ashwagandha cure nerve damage?

No, ashwagandha isn't a treatment for nerve damage. It's traditionally valued as a nerve-calming tonic, and animal studies are exploring its protective potential, but nerve damage needs proper medical care.

Is ashwagandha banned in India?

No, the root is permitted. In April 2026, India's Ministry of AYUSH restricted AYUSH products to the root, and FSSAI advised against leaves in food products. Some manufacturers have challenged this in court.

What organ is ashwagandha hard on?

The liver, in rare cases. Liver injury has been linked to ashwagandha supplements, and it may also affect thyroid function. Most people tolerate it well, but stop and seek help if symptoms appear.

What will happen if I take ashwagandha daily?

Taken daily for a few weeks, ashwagandha may help some people manage stress and sleep better. It's generally well tolerated for up to three months, though mild stomach upset or drowsiness can occur.
